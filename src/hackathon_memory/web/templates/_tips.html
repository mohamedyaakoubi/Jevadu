The closest project can stand in another group: a group is tight as a whole, so a project at its edge can be closer to one project of another group. A strong line joins projects with the same code or the same Hedera ID, as in Insights.
  • Zoom: hold Ctrl and scroll over the graph, or pinch on a touchpad; then drag it, or scroll with two fingers, to move it. The buttons zoom in, out and back. The dots keep their size, so dots that touch come apart.

{% endcall %} {% call tip("proof", "Hedera proof") %}

Did the project really use Hedera? The parser finds the Hedera IDs on the project page and in its README: accounts{{ parts.ref("account") }}, topics{{ parts.ref("topic") }}, tokens{{ parts.ref("token") }}, smart contracts{{ parts.ref("contract") }} and single transactions. Each one is looked up on the public mirror node{{ parts.ref("mirror") }}.

  • Found: the ledger knows the ID as that kind of entity, created or used during the event.
  • Dates don't fit: it exists, but from another time.
  • Wrong kind of ID: the ledger knows the number as another kind of entity.
  • Empty ID: nothing ever happened with it, or the transaction failed.
  • ID not found: the ledger does not know it.
  • No ID given: neither the page nor the README names one. That is common.
  • Found in the code: the code of the project names one of the team's own IDs, which no other team names, made on the ledger during the event, such as the account that runs its program. Code also names IDs that many teams use, such as a public token, so the code only counts in this strict way.

Every ID links to HashScan{{ parts.ref("explorer") }}, a public explorer of the same records.

{% endcall %} {% call tip("winner", "Prize") %}
  • Won a Hedera prize: the projects that the organisers gave a Hedera prize: on ETHGlobal, the block "Winner of" on the project's own page; on Devfolio, a prize won in a track of Hedera; for the other hackathons, the organisers' lists of winners.
  • Won another sponsor's prize: the projects that took a prize of another sponsor at their hackathon, and no Hedera prize: the block "Winner of" on the project's own page, or the prizes that a page of Devfolio lists, name that sponsor.
  • Winners' list only: among the Hedera prizes, the projects that are known only from such a list, because their own page is gone or was never public. They have a name and a line, no full card.
{% endcall %} {% call tip("fresh", "Read again") %}

So GitHub and the mirror node are asked again once their answers are a day old{% if REFRESH_IN_BACKGROUND %}, while the website runs{% else %}, when the collection is read again{% endif %}: the code, the IDs on the ledger and how they are set up, their new transactions, and what the calls of its contracts reached.

{% endcall %} {% call tip("life", "Activity after the event") %}

Was the project still worked on after the event? Activity means a commit, or on-chain activity of the project's own IDs, more than 30 days after the event.

  • Active: activity in the last 90 days.
  • Quiet: activity, but none in the last 90 days.
  • Stopped: nothing more than 30 days after the event.

{% endcall %} {% call tip("story", "Hedera services: page, code and ledger") %}

For each Hedera service, what the project page says, what the code calls, and what its own IDs did on the ledger:

  • Page: the words of the project page that name the service, such as “HCS” or “topic ID” for the Consensus Service{{ parts.ref("topic") }}, “HTS” for the Token Service{{ parts.ref("token") }}, or “smart contract” and “Solidity” for smart contracts{{ parts.ref("contract") }}.   • Code: whether a source file names one of the service's SDK classes, such as TokenCreateTransaction. A package alone does not count. “EVM setup” means that the code points EVM tools such as Hardhat at Hedera, which is not a call by itself.
  • Ledger: the transactions of its own Hedera IDs, read from a mirror node{{ parts.ref("mirror") }}, from 30 days before the event to 30 days after it.
  • From its contracts: the calls of its own smart contracts that reached one of Hedera's system contracts, which give a contract the Token Service, the Account Service, the Schedule Service, random numbers and the exchange rate{{ parts.ref("system") }}. They count for their service beside the transactions. Of a contract with many calls, the first 100 in that time are read.

Its own IDs are those whose kind fits the page and that no other team names: an ID that several teams name is often a shared service. A public service that its owner lists, such as the price oracle of Pyth or Circle's USDC, is named on the card with its source, and is never counted as the project's own. The entries of one team's code at several hackathons share their IDs. The page is not checked for HBAR, since nearly every project moves HBAR to set itself up. A team can also use a service through an ID that its page does not give.

  • The days: each square is a day with transactions, darker for more. Point at one, or reach it with the keyboard, to see its number; a click opens its first transaction on HashScan{{ parts.ref("explorer") }}. Before and after that time, only its topics, tokens, contracts and schedules count: an account often lives on for years and across projects. The strip draws their days too, however far from the event; a hatched band is a time of more than 30 days with none, left out, and its length stands under it. Anyone can send a transaction to a contract, a topic or a token, so the lines under the strip say who paid for those transactions: accounts that the page names, or others, who may be strangers.
  • Who took part: the accounts that the page does not name, in its transfers, its messages and the calls of its contracts, and the accounts that hold its tokens now. One of them can be the team's own, such as a test account. The network's own accounts are left out.
  • Who made its IDs: for each own contract, topic, token and schedule, the account that paid for the transaction that created it, and when, read from the mirror node. One made more than 30 days before the event, by an account that made none of its other IDs and that the project does not list, may belong to someone else, such as a public service.
  • How they are set up: what the mirror node said on the day of the check, since keys and fees can change. A token is fungible or a collection of NFTs, with a supply limit or none; its keys are what can still act on it (admin, supply, freeze, KYC, wipe, pause, fee schedule, metadata); its custom fees are those that Hedera itself takes on each transfer for the token's collector: a fixed fee, a share of a transfer, or a royalty on the sale of an NFT{{ parts.ref("fees") }}. A topic takes messages from anyone, or only from the holder of its submit key, and an admin key can change or delete it{{ parts.ref("topickeys") }}. An account has one key, ED25519 or ECDSA, the kind that EVM wallets use, or a key made of several keys{{ parts.ref("keys") }}.
  • Signs that stay on paper: a topic with no message, a token held only by the project's own accounts, a contract never called, accounts that only moved HBAR, a Hedera package that nothing calls, or IDs that other projects name too.

{% endcall %} {% call tip("hbar", "HBAR: not checked") %}

Nearly every project moves HBAR, the coin of Hedera, just to set itself up: to create its accounts{{ parts.ref("account") }} and to pay the fees of its transactions. Whether a page or a README names HBAR says nothing about how a project uses Hedera, so it is not checked.

{% endcall %} {% call tip("hedera-code", "Hedera in the code") %}

We read every file of the project's own code once, from the archive of its default branch on GitHub{{ parts.ref("repository") }}, and keep what we find: the Hedera packages that its package files name, the Hedera services that it calls (by the class names of Hedera's SDKs{{ parts.ref("hedera") }}, such as TopicCreateTransaction), other signs such as Hedera's JSON-RPC relay, a Hedera chain ID, a Hedera network of viem, wagmi or Hardhat, Hedera's system contracts or the mirror node{{ parts.ref("mirror") }}, the files that import a Hedera library, and the files that only name Hedera. Each file opens on GitHub as it was read.

Folders of other people's code, such as node_modules, are skipped, and .env files are never opened. This shows what the code names, not whether it runs, and it cannot see Hedera used from another repository.

{% endcall %} {% call tip("check", "Check a submission") %}

For judges, or for a team before it submits. Enter the GitHub repository, and the hackathon if you want the dates checked.

  • The check asks GitHub's API for the repository, its README, its newest commit and the archive of its code, one request after the other. An answer less than an hour old is used again.
  • It finds the Hedera IDs in the README, looks each one up on the public mirror node{{ parts.ref("mirror") }}, and checks the dates against the hackathon.
  • It reads the code for Hedera: its packages, its imports and its calls to Hedera services. A repository that was never read takes a few seconds, at most 30.
  • It shows whether the same repository or ID appeared at an earlier event, and the most similar past projects.

You can then register the submission on Hedera{{ parts.ref("topic") }}.

{% endcall %} {% call tip("seen", "Linked projects") %}

Two projects are linked when they share a repository{{ parts.ref("repository") }}, or a Hedera ID on the same network that the ledger confirmed for both. Exact matching.

  • An ID that many teams use is most likely a shared service, such as a common test account or a public payment address: more than three different repositories in all, or two at one hackathon. Such an ID links nothing.
  • An account alone makes no timeline: a builder often keeps one account for every project, so two projects with one account share a builder, not necessarily a project. The link says so.
  • A shared name links nothing either: two teams can pick the same name.
{% endcall %} {% call tip("closest", "Most similar past projects") %}

The past projects closest in meaning to the README, found with the same semantic search as an idea (Google's embedding model{{ parts.ref("embedding") }}). Similar does not mean copied.

{% endcall %} {% call tip("register", "Public register") %}

A list of records in one topic{{ parts.ref("topic") }} on Hedera testnet{{ parts.ref("testnet") }}. Each record says: this repository was registered for this hackathon. Hedera stamps it with a sequence number and a consensus time that nobody can change.

  • The topic has no admin key, so nobody can delete the register or change its rules, this site included. Only the site's own account can write to it.
  • A record holds a 64-character fingerprint of the repository link, and no link: the same link always gives the same fingerprint, and the fingerprint does not reveal the link. A signed record also holds the account that signed, by the team's choice.
  • It shows when a repository was registered. When the same repository comes back for another hackathon, the check shows the first date.
  • A team can sign its record with its own Hedera account, through a Hedera wallet such as HashPack, or an EVM wallet such as MetaMask. The record then holds the account and the signature, which anybody can check against the account's key on the ledger. The site's own account still writes the record and pays for it.

The list shows the hackathons running now first, then past ones by the year they ended. Registering on Hedera needs a hackathon from the list.
